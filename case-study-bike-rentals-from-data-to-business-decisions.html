Chapter 2 Case Study: Bike Rentals — From Data to Business Decisions

2.1 The situation

Imagine that you are working as a junior data analyst for a bike-sharing company.

The company operates a large fleet of rental bikes in a city. Every day, thousands of customers use the service for commuting, leisure, shopping and other activities.

However, demand is not constant.

Some days, many more bikes are rented than on others. Demand appears to depend on factors such as:

  • temperature and weather,
  • rainfall,
  • season,
  • day of the week,
  • whether it is a working day or a weekend,
  • and other factors that are not directly observed in the data.

The company currently has a practical problem:

It needs to decide how many bikes should be available at a certain rental location, but tomorrow’s demand is uncertain.

If too few bikes are available, customers cannot rent a bike and the company loses potential revenue.

If too many bikes are available in the wrong locations, the company incurs unnecessary costs for redistribution, maintenance and unused capacity.

The company therefore wants to use its historical data to understand and predict rental demand.

Aim: Using historical bike-rental data and the statistical tools developed in this course, make an evidence-based recommendation about tomorrow’s bike demand and quantify the uncertainty surrounding your recommendation.

The practical exercises throughout the semester provide the statistical tools needed to answer this question.

The final business problem therefore brings together:

descriptive statistics → association → regression → randomness → conditional probability → independence → random variables → probability distributions → parameter estimation.

Data analysis with statistical methods:

  • Confirm or specify assumptions with data

  • Uncover new correlations, determine influencing factors

  • (Further) developing products/advertising, modeling behavior, predicting business development, …

Definition 2.1 (Key Concepts)

  • \(\Omega\): Population, is a set of uniform objects, also referred to as statistical units \(\omega\in\Omega\), which are characterized by the same characteristics.
  • \(X\): feature or characteristic, is an observable property of the elements of \(\Omega\). Each feature can take on different feature values.
  • \(A_X\): Set of values of the characteristic \(X\).
  • \(X_1, X_2, \ldots , X_n\): Sample is the random selection of objects from the population. After drawing a sample, the measurement series (the determination of the respective characteristics of one or more features) is created on the randomly selected objects.
    • \(n\): sample size
    • \(x_i\): \(i\)-th sample value/ measurement

Example 2.1 (Sampling in a customer survey) The plan is to randomly select \(n\) customers and survey them. The following characteristics are of interest:

  • App Usage:

The sample values are: \(\color{blue}{y_1, y_2,\ldots,y_n},\) where \(y_i\) represents the app usage of the \(i\)th selected person.

  • Sales:

The sample values are: \({\color{red}{x_1, x_2,\ldots,x_n}},\) where \(x_i\) represents the estimate of the sales amount of the \(i\)th selected person.

Sample size \(n\):

Sample realizations:

  • The survey results for App usage (yes/no) as realized sample values:

    \(\color{blue}{\{1,2,\ldots, n\}=} ~\)

  • As realizations of the corresponding 0-1 Bernoulli random variable:

    \(\color{blue}{\{y_1,y_2,\ldots,y_n\}=}~\)

  • Willingness to pay in form of self-estimated sales amount with sample values:

    \(\color{red}{\{x_1,x_2,\ldots,x_n\}=}~\)

Calculation of the resulting estimates:

  • The estimate for the proportion of app users:

    \(\color{blue}{\bar y=\frac 1n(y_1+y_2 + \ldots + y_n)=}\)

  • The estimate for the average sales amount:

    \(\color{red}{\bar x=\frac 1n(x_1+x_2 + \ldots + x_n)= }\)
